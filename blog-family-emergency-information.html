What Information Should Your Family Know in an Emergency?

The goal is not to predict every emergency. It is to make sure a trusted person has a sensible starting point when you are not available to explain where everything is.

Open Theory Cove · September 26, 2026 · About 7 minutes

Start with the questions another person would have

If you normally manage the household details, imagine someone responsible and trustworthy had to step in tomorrow. Who would they call first? Which people or animals depend on you? Where would they find important records? What bills, property, accounts, care instructions, or appointments could not simply be ignored?

You do not need one enormous file containing every secret. What you need is a reliable map: enough information to show an authorized, trusted person what exists, what needs attention, and where the appropriate records or access instructions are kept.

A practical emergency-information checklist

Key people & contacts

Family, dependents, emergency contacts, caregivers, employers when relevant, doctors, veterinarians, attorneys, financial professionals, insurance contacts, and anyone else who may need to be reached.

Identification & records

Where important identification, certificates, insurance records, titles, deeds, tax records, and other essential documents are stored.

Medical & care information

Providers, medications or medication-list location, allergies or important health information you choose to share, and where advance directives or other existing care documents can be found.

Home, property & vehicles

Property details, vehicles, valuables, household responsibilities, maintenance needs, keys, and where related records are kept.

Financial responsibilities

Which institutions and recurring obligations exist, where records are stored, and how an authorized person can find the information they are legally entitled to use.

Digital life

Important devices, online services, subscriptions, digital assets, and where secure access instructions are kept.

Pets & dependents

Daily routines, medications, food, school or care contacts, veterinarian information, preferences, and the people who could step in.

Legal documents & wishes

Where existing wills, powers of attorney, advance directives, trusts, beneficiary information, and other formal documents can be found.

Do not turn your binder into a security problem

Convenience and security need to coexist. A binder or workbook that lists every password, PIN, alarm code, full account number, and security answer can create a new risk if it is lost or seen by the wrong person.

A safer principle: use your organizer as a map. Identify what exists and, when appropriate, where authorized access information is securely stored rather than copying every sensitive credential into an unsecured book.

Make the information usable

A perfect organizer that nobody knows exists is not very helpful. Tell at least one trusted person that you have organized the information and where the organizer can be found. Keep formal legal documents where they belong; the organizer can point to their location rather than trying to replace them.

Review your information periodically and after meaningful changes such as moving, changing providers, opening or closing important accounts, adding a pet, changing emergency contacts, or updating legal documents.

You can build it gradually

Start with the easiest category. Completing even one useful section is better than postponing the entire project because you cannot finish it in a weekend. Each unanswered question can become a small task: find the insurance policy, update the veterinarian number, identify where a title is stored, or ask a professional about a document you may need.

Important: This article and the related workbook are organizational resources, not legal, medical, financial, or estate-planning advice. Formal documents and professional advice should come from appropriately qualified professionals.
