How to Figure Out What You Want to Do With Your Life When You Honestly Don't Know

You do not need to discover the one thing you are supposed to do forever. You need enough evidence about yourself, the life you want, and the real world to choose a direction worth testing next.

Open Theory Cove · September 26, 2026 · Practical guide
The short version: Stop beginning with job titles. Start with what gives and takes your energy, the strengths you actually want to use, the life your work needs to support, and the things you never want to repeat. Then generate possibilities, investigate what the work is really like, test a few in small ways, and choose the next direction—not a permanent identity.

1. Start with what is true now

When you feel lost, the instinct is often to search lists of careers and hope one produces an immediate feeling of certainty. A better starting point is your own evidence. Your past work, responsibilities, hobbies, frustrations, good days, bad days, and ordinary routines already contain information about fit.

Begin with two lists: what you never want again and what you would keep. Think beyond employers. Consider pace, schedule, physical demands, people contact, independence, leadership, repetition, pressure, location, flexibility, and the kind of problems you were expected to solve.

Try this

Write down three moments from recent life or work that gave you energy and three that drained it. For each one, ask: What exactly was happening? Look for the ingredient, not just whether the day was “good” or “bad.”

2. Look for strengths you overlook

People routinely dismiss abilities that come naturally to them. If organizing a mess, calming an upset customer, spotting an error, explaining a confusing process, caring for animals, making something attractive, or figuring out unfamiliar software feels ordinary to you, you may not count it as a skill. Other people may experience it very differently.

Ask better questions about your abilities

  • What do people repeatedly ask me to help with?
  • What have difficult experiences taught me to do well?
  • What do I learn unusually quickly?
  • What kind of work makes me lose track of time?
  • Which strengths do I actually enjoy using?

The three-person experiment

Ask three people who know you in different settings: “What are three things you think I am naturally good at?” Do not argue with their answers. Write down their exact words and look for repetition.

3. Design the life before the job

A career is only one part of a life. A job can sound impressive and still be a poor fit if its schedule, income, location, stress, travel, physical demands, or lack of flexibility conflicts with the life you need or want.

Imagine an ordinary Tuesday—not a vacation and not a fantasy version of success. When do you wake up? Where do you work? How much interaction do you want? How much time do you need for family, rest, interests, or other responsibilities? What level of income do you realistically need?

Create three lists

Must have: genuine requirements. Prefer: things that would improve the fit. Doesn't matter: things you can be flexible about. Include income, hours, location, remote/on-site work, travel, physical demands, benefits, stability, autonomy, and people contact.

Knowing your minimum acceptable income is particularly important. Money is not the only consideration, but ignoring it can turn an interesting path into an unsustainable one.

4. Generate possibilities before judging them

Do not demand that every idea immediately prove it can become your next career. That shuts down exploration too early. Create a possibility parking lot instead: careers, industries, businesses, specialties, projects, training programs, or combinations that make you curious.

Use clues from childhood interests, things you read about voluntarily, problems you enjoy solving, and even envy. If someone's working life makes you think, “I wish I could do that,” ask what you actually want from it. It may be creativity, independence, income, recognition, travel, flexibility, meaningful work, or control over your time rather than that person's exact occupation.

Push past the obvious

Try to reach twenty possibilities. Include combinations. A satisfying working life does not have to fit inside one title. A stable job plus a creative business, part-time employment plus caregiving, or two related income streams can also be a direction.

5. Research the reality behind the title

Once you have possibilities, curiosity has to meet reality. Job titles can be misleading. What matters is what people actually do all day and what it takes to enter the field.

For each serious possibility, investigate the daily duties, entry requirements, realistic pay at different experience levels, schedule, travel, physical demands, stress, flexibility, and current opportunities. Look at multiple real job postings. Separate requirements from preferences. Search for ordinary day-in-the-life accounts, not only promotional descriptions.

Use a reality-check sheet

For each possibility, record: daily work · income · entry cost · time to qualify · schedule · location · physical demands · stress · flexibility · opportunity · what attracts me · what concerns me.

A possibility becoming less appealing after research is not a failure. That is the process working.

6. Test before you commit

Thinking can only take you so far. Before investing heavily in a degree, certification, equipment, relocation, or business, look for the smallest realistic version of the experience.

  • Take a free beginner lesson.
  • Complete a tiny project that resembles the work.
  • Talk with someone who actually does it.
  • Attend an industry event or class.
  • Volunteer or shadow where appropriate.
  • Try a freelance, hobby, or simulated version of the task.

Pay attention not only to whether you are immediately good at it. Notice whether you want to understand more, whether the frustrating parts feel tolerable, and whether your interest survives contact with the ordinary parts of the work.

The Monday-morning test

Imagine the path is no longer new or impressive. It is Monday morning and this is simply your job. Write a realistic schedule for the day. Which parts are you looking forward to? Which are you already dreading?

7. Choose a direction—not forever

Eventually, exploration can become another way to avoid deciding. The goal is not perfect certainty. It is enough evidence to choose a direction that deserves focused effort next.

Compare your strongest possibilities on interest, income, lifestyle fit, entry difficulty, stability, flexibility, growth, and the actual daily work. Then consider what a score cannot measure: which path you would regret never testing, which obstacles are real, and which are assumptions or fears that can be investigated.

Make a provisional decision

Finish this sentence: “For the next 90 days, I am going to seriously explore ______.”

Then choose three actions with dates. Examples might include completing a course, talking with two people in the field, building a sample project, updating a résumé or portfolio, applying for a defined number of positions, or testing a small paid version of an idea.

You are allowed to change your mind

A direction is not a life sentence. New evidence can change it. You may discover that an old dream no longer fits, that something you dismissed suits you surprisingly well, or that the life you want matters more than a particular title.

The useful question is not always, “What am I supposed to do with my life?” A more workable question is: “What direction has enough evidence behind it that I am willing to explore it next?”

That question is smaller. It is also something you can answer.
