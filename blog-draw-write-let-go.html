Draw It, Write It, Let It Go

Sometimes your thoughts are too tangled for a neat journal entry. When that happens, start with an image instead of a sentence.

You do not need to know how to draw. This exercise is not about making art. It is about giving your mind another way to speak. A scribble, a storm cloud, a maze, a face, a pile of boxes or a page full of arrows can sometimes reveal what you are feeling before you have words for it.

A Three-Step Reset for a Busy Mind

1

Draw It

Sketch what your mind feels like right now. Use shapes, symbols, lines, objects or complete nonsense. Do not correct it and do not try to make it attractive.

2

Write It

Look at what you drew and write whatever comes up. What does the image represent? What thought keeps returning? What feels unfinished, exciting, funny or heavy?

3

Let It Go

Circle one thing you want to keep. Underline one thing you want to change. Cross out one thing you are ready to stop carrying today.

Why Combine Drawing and Writing?

Words are useful, but they are not always the fastest route to what is really going on. Drawing gives you permission to be less organized. Writing then helps you notice what the picture brought to the surface. The final step turns the page into a small decision: what stays with you, what needs attention and what can be released for now.

You can spend five minutes on this or fill several pages. There is no right outcome. The goal is simply to move some of the noise from your head onto paper.

Six Prompts for a Beautifully Chaotic Mind

  1. If my mind had a weather forecast today, what would it be?
  2. What is something I keep overthinking that might be simpler than it feels?
  3. What deserves more of my attention — and what deserves less?
  4. Draw the thought taking up the most space in your head.
  5. What would I say if I knew no one would judge the answer?
  6. What is one thought I can leave on this page instead of carrying with me?

Try answering with words one day and pictures the next. You may notice that different forms of expression reveal different parts of the same thought.
