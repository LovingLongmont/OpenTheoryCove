Creative Journaling

Words • Lines • Connection

Some thoughts become clearer when you write them down. Others make more sense when you draw them. This simple three-step practice uses both, giving the same idea two different ways to speak.

Why use both writing and sketching?

Writing encourages you to name, explain and organize. Sketching can do something different: it lets you notice shape, distance, movement, tension, repetition and emotion without needing the right words first. You do not need to be an artist. A few lines, symbols or boxes can be enough.

The goal is not to create a polished page. The goal is to see whether a thought changes when you move it from sentences into images and back again.

1. Words

Choose one thought, problem, memory or idea. Write about it for a few minutes without editing yourself. Let the page hold the full version, including contradictions.

2. Lines

Now represent the same idea visually. Draw a place, map, object, symbol, path, collection of shapes or even a messy pattern. Use whatever comes naturally.

3. Connection

Compare the two pages. What appeared in the drawing that you never wrote? What did the writing explain that the sketch could not? Finish with one sentence about what you see now.

Try these six prompts

  1. Write about where your attention keeps going today, then sketch it as a place.
  2. List three things competing for your energy, then turn them into shapes on a blank page.
  3. Write one memory in five sentences, then draw the single detail you remember most clearly.
  4. Describe something you want more of, then draw the feeling rather than the object.
  5. Write about a decision you are considering, then sketch two possible paths forward.
  6. Describe how today feels in words, then give that feeling a symbol you can recognize later.

What to notice afterward

You may find that the writing is more logical while the drawing is more emotional. You may discover that one small detail keeps showing up. Or you may simply feel less crowded because the thought is no longer only in your head. Any of those outcomes can be useful.

You can repeat this practice with the same topic several days later and compare the pages. The goal is not to arrive at a perfect answer; it is to create another way of seeing.
